What this is

A small, source-available tool that answers questions about your journal.

Runs on your computer, with your own API key — no account, nothing in between. Export from Day One or Diarium, point this at the file, start asking.

Local by design
Free to download, API cost only
Source available
Not a Day One or Diarium user? This might still work for you.

The pipeline under the hood — embed text, store it locally, retrieve the relevant pieces, ask an LLM — doesn't care where the text came from. If you journal in another app (Notion, Obsidian, Apple Notes, Google Keep, Penzu, Journey, Grid Diary, or even plain text files) and can export your entries as JSON or plain text, the retrieval and chat layers would work with it. It's not a polished integration the way Day One is, but if you're comfortable adapting code, the bones are there.

What it actually does

Built for the questions a plain search box can't answer.

Ask it anything

"What was I feeling last spring?" or "Detail my trip to California" — asked in plain English, answered from entries that are actually relevant, not a keyword match.

Max Recall

Toggle it on for "everything about X" questions, and it pulls every tagged entry on that topic — not just the handful that rank highest.

nothing gets left out

Needs tagging turned on first — optional, and off by default.

Photo search

Finds photos by what's visually in them, using CLIP — "show me the beach photo" can work even if you never wrote the word "beach."

Remembers the conversation

Follow-ups like "tell me more about that" work naturally, across turns — it's a conversation, not a search bar you retype every time.

Mood over time

A monthly sentiment chart built from your own words, with no extra work — a byproduct of the same pass that indexes everything else.

Runs where you already are

A local web app, phone-friendly over Tailscale, installable to your home screen — feels like an app, stays entirely on hardware you own.

What it costs

Not free to run — but we don't take a cut.

This project itself is free and we don't earn anything from it. But using it isn't free: asking questions and tagging entries both call an AI provider's API, and that provider bills you directly for it — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, or Mistral, whichever you pick in the setup wizard. Every one of those dollars goes to them, none of it to us. We'd rather say that plainly here than let "free" be mistaken for "no cost to run."

  • Google (gemini-2.0-flash): $0.15 / $0.60 per M tokens — ~$0.05
  • Mistral (mistral-small): $0.15 / $0.60 per M tokens — ~$0.05
  • Anthropic (claude-haiku-4-5): $1.00 / $5.00 per M tokens — ~$0.35–$0.40

Claude Haiku is Anthropic's cheapest model, but it's still roughly 7–8x pricier per token than the cheap tier from OpenAI, Google, or Mistral — so if minimizing this specific cost is your priority, Haiku isn't actually the cheapest option overall, just the cheapest Anthropic one. Setup

No coding required — here's the whole path.

Your journal has to get from the app you wrote it in, onto the computer that'll answer questions about it. That's really the whole job. Here's what that looks like end to end — a setup page handles the technical middle part for you, from your browser, and once it's done, staying up to date needs no further clicks.

1

Export your journal

Open Day One or Diarium and use its export option to save your entries as a file. How easy this is depends on which app and device you use — see Before you export below.

2

Get that file onto this computer

Already journaling on this computer? Skip this one. Exported from your phone instead? Point the setup wizard at a cloud-sync folder — Dropbox, iCloud, OneDrive, whatever you already use — and exports dropped there are picked up and ingested automatically from then on. Emailing the file to yourself or a cable both still work fine too, just as one-off manual copies.

3

Double-click the launcher

start_setup.bat on Windows, start_setup.command on Mac. It checks for Python, helps you get it if you don't have it, and opens the setup page automatically.

4

Paste in an API key

A couple of minutes with whichever AI provider you choose — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, or Mistral. The setup page can install the required packages for you too — entirely optional, one click.

5

Drop the file in, and ask away

Put the export in the folder you chose and start asking — the setup wizard already registered a watcher that checks for new exports every 60 seconds and ingests anything new by itself, so there's no command to run here. Full walkthrough is in the repo's README.

Before you export

Step 1 (exporting) is easy for some, a bit of a chore for others.

The export itself is entirely up to Day One or Diarium, and the two behave pretty differently. Here's what to expect from each:

If you use Day One

There is no automatic sync. Because of limitations with Day One’s export system, uploads can’t be fully automatic. The closest thing: iPhone’s date-range export + a one-tap Shortcut that drops it into your sync folder. About 10 seconds, and that’s as seamless as it gets.

Every platform can export, but not every platform can export just the new entries:

iPhone
Best. Date range + tag filtering — export just entries since your last export.
Android
Good. Date range filtering — export a specific range of entries.
Mac
OK. Tag filtering + manual Cmd/Shift+click in list view to select entries — no date range picker.
Windows / Web
Full only. No filtering at all — every export is your entire journal. (Export is in Journal Settings, not the main app settings.)

What this means for you:

Mac + iPhone
Smoothest combo. iPhone for quick date-range exports (since last time), Mac when you need a full export or want to hand-pick entries. Day-to-day, mostly export from your iPhone.
Mac + Android
Similar — date-range exports from Android for ongoing updates, Mac for a full export when needed.
Windows + iPhone
Your iPhone is your best friend — Windows can only do full re-exports, so export from iPhone for ongoing use.
Windows + Android
Same idea — date-range exports from Android, since Windows can’t filter at all.
Windows only
Works, but every export is the full journal. Fine to start; slower for ongoing updates.
Technical details

This project needs a JSON export file from Day One. Day One syncs your journal across devices even on the free plan, so you can export from whichever device is most convenient.

On iPhone, Day One lets you set a date range and filter by tag before exporting — so you can export just entries since your last export. Android offers date range filtering too. On Mac, there’s no date picker, but you can filter by tag and manually select entries in list view (Cmd+click or Shift+click) then right-click → Export. Windows and the web app export your entire journal only — the option is under Edit Journal → Journal Settings, not the main app settings.

If you do re-export your whole journal: this project hashes each entry and only re-processes ones that changed, so a repeated full export is safe and cheap — it just means the export step itself takes longer than a quick filtered one would.

If you use Diarium

Exports your whole journal every time, on every device — and the file format isn't officially documented, so this project makes its best guess at reading it, and says clearly if it can't.

Its own sync is continuous through your own cloud folder, so there's no export-and-reupload dance the way there is with Day One — the catch is Cloud Sync needs a separate paid upgrade on each platform (Windows ships with it included; iPhone, Android, and Mac each need their own), and Google Drive is the slowest of its supported backends, if you get to pick.

Technical details

Diarium doesn't publish a schema for its JSON export, and based on its own community forum, exporting a specific date range or tag doesn't currently appear to be a built-in option — a full export looks to be the normal path today. Diarium's own developer has also confirmed the app can't re-import its own JSON export, so there's no strict format being maintained internally to check against either.

This project's Diarium support is genuinely best-effort: it tries several plausible field names rather than one guess, and fails with a clear, specific error if your export doesn't match — rather than a silent wrong answer. If it doesn't work for you, a GitHub issue with a small redacted sample helps get it fixed for real.

Full details, including exact export steps for both apps and both mobile and desktop, are in the README.

A quiet note on privacy

Your journal stays on your machine, and only the few lines relevant to a question ever leave it.

There's no account, no sync service, and the page that holds your API key only ever listens on your own computer — never your phone, your network, or anywhere else.
